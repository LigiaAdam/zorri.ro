How to measure and engineer AI repeatability

Same input, same-ish answer from AI, every time. How ReLoop.me benchmarked output consistency, published the results, and engineered out probabilistic drift.

By Ligia Adam, founder of ZORRI · 27 November 2026

The challenge: when the same input produces a different verdict

First, some context

ReLoop.me evaluates CVs and job descriptions to produce structured capability intelligence (Profile Intel and Role Intel), including role classification, seniority grading and market compensation signals.

The problem?

General-purpose AI models are inherently probabilistic.

Ask any chat AI to evaluate the exact same CV twice, and it can return a different seniority tier, or a wildly different salary estimate, on the second try.

A verdict that shifts on a re-run undermines trust, and cannot support a real decision.

When people rely on an assessment to negotiate pay or evaluate talent, that matters.

To make ReLoop.me reliable enough for high-stakes career and hiring decisions, we needed to measure how often the engine gave the same verdict twice, and then engineer away and fix the parts that moved.

The solution

What we built

A fixed, standardised vocabulary

When asked, AI uses a lot of different words to refer to the same thing.

So we replaced open-ended AI labelling with a strict taxonomy: seven role types, a single standardised seniority ladder, and explicitly defined capability levels that the model must map to, rather than invent.

Choosing and settling on the final vocabulary took deeper research across a wide range of topics. Eventually, the fixed vocabulary became a key part of the engine's proprietary framework.

Deterministic, calculated pay logic

Generic AI is probabilistic. It will give you the average, most recent values of whatever it weights most when evaluating the data. We approached capability market value differently.

We decoupled compensation figures from generative guessing.

Inside ReLoop.me, pay bands are pulled programmatically from structured lookup tables indexed by role family, location and seniority tier, each explicitly labelled as Calibrated or Estimated.

This way, the deterministic part works across nonlinear careers as well. For tailored, not averaged, measures.

An automated AI drift test

Drift is common with AI. But what is drifting needs constant monitoring, especially when it can affect the rest of the moving pieces.

So we built an internal, automated evaluation suite that runs the exact same CV or job ad through the pipeline 8 to 30 consecutive times, and programmatically compares the outputs to measure drift across every field.

This helps with internal calibration and continuous improvement.

Radical transparency, via a public methodology page

At ZORRI, we believe AI governance needs human accountability.

So we publish the results of the repeatability tests we run, including where variance still occurs, at reloop.me/methodology.

Results

Self-run repeat-run tests on production settings, 1 October 2026: one CV and one job ad, 30 runs each, against two general-purpose models. Published at reloop.me/methodology:

  • 30 / 30role type, identical on the CV and on the job ad
  • 30 / 30CV seniority, identical. The two general-purpose models shifted verdict in 6 and 3 of 30 runs
  • 93%of job-ad capabilities kept the same band across repeat runs, against 87% and 92% for the two general-purpose models
  • Role Quick Check: matched the expected answer in 24 of 24 runs on a fixed set of 12 job ads in October, and in 23 of 24 reads in the November re-test (ReLoop.me Benchmark Notes No. 1).
  • No echo of self-labels: when a CV rated its own capabilities, those self-ratings appeared in 0 of 8 Profile Intel runs, against 4 to 8 of 8 for general-purpose models.

Post-calibration improvements

Internal remeasurement on synthetic CVs, 21 November 2026:

  • Pay spread on the same CV: reduced from 34% to 4%.
  • Seniority stability against title inflation: stable in 3 of 3 runs. Changing a job title alone no longer moves the assessed seniority level.
  • Resistance to framing bias: adding outside recruiter context shifted pay estimates by just −0.9%, down from +17%.

What still varies inside the ReLoop.me engine

Running a live, AI-powered system means constantly adapting to change. A lot of things vary, and we found it's best to keep checking.

The most recent tests show that, on genuinely ambiguous job ads, seniority grading and which of two role types leads can still vary between full runs. On the 1 October job ad, seniority read Senior Manager in 22 of 30 runs and Senior in 8.

Natural-language phrasing also shifts from run to run: the wording of a capability changes even when its level and band hold. That's why the methodology page advises leaning on the level and band, not the exact phrase.

Both limitations are documented openly on the methodology page, and we're working on them.
